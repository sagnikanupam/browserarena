📄 Result: The Great Wall of China traces its origins to a patchwork of frontier defenses built by regional states during the Spring and Autumn (771–476 BC) and Warring States (475–221 BC) periods. In 221 BC, China’s first emperor, Qin Shi Huang, ordered these walls linked into a continuous barrier of rammed earth stretching from today’s Gansu Province to Manchuria to fend off nomadic incursions.

Over succeeding centuries, various dynasties adopted and rebuilt the Wall. The Han dynasty (202 BC–220 AD) extended it farthest west. The Northern Qi (550–574 AD) added roughly 1,600 km of new fortifications, and the Sui (581–618 AD) mobilized over a million workers on wall projects. During the Tang (618–907), Song (960–1279), Yuan (1271–1368), and Qing (1644–1912) dynasties, China relied more on military campaigns and diplomacy than on wall building, so little new construction took place.

The most familiar sections visible today date from the Ming dynasty (1368–1644). Ming emperors rebuilt and reinforced the Wall in brick and stone, often rerouting it through rugged terrain and manning it with permanent garrisons. Despite its strength, it failed to prevent the Manchu invasion through Shanhai Pass in 1644.

After the Qing united China, parts of the Wall fell into disrepair or were dismantled for building materials. In the 20th and 21st centuries, the Wall has been restored in places, studied by historians, and become a symbol of China’s heritage and a prime tourist attraction.
