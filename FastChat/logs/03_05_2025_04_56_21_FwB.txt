**Overview**: The history of the Great Wall began with fortifications by various states during the Spring and Autumn (771–476 BC) and Warring States periods (475–221 BC). These were connected by Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of China, to protect the Qin dynasty (221–206 BC) from nomadic incursions. Significant rebuilding and expansion occurred during the Han (202 BC – 220 AD), Northern Qi (550–574), Jin (1115–1234), and Ming (1369–1644) dynasties. The wall visible today mostly dates from the Ming dynasty, made of stone and brick. Despite its purpose, it failed notably in 1644 when Qing troops passed through Shanhai Pass, ending Ming rule. The total length is approximately 21,196 km as per a 2012 survey.

**Geographical Context**: The Wall's construction was influenced by the divide between China’s agricultural south and the semi-arid grasslands of Inner Asia, with key defense areas like Mongolia, Manchuria, and Xinjiang. Natural barriers like the Yin Mountains aided defense, but strategic areas like the Ordos Loop and Hexi Corridor required extensive fortifications.

**Historical Phases**:
- **Pre-imperial China (7th Century–221 BC)**: Early walls were built by states like Chu, Qi, Wei, and Qin against northern invaders, often of tamped earth, for territorial security.
- **Qin Dynasty (221–206 BC)**: Qin Shi Huang connected existing walls into a barrier from Gansu to Manchuria by 212 BC using forced labor.
- **Han Dynasty (206 BC–220 AD)**: Extended walls to the west under Emperor Wu during the Han–Xiongnu War, using military colonies.
- **Period of Disunity to Sui Dynasty (220–618)**: Northern Wei, Qi, and Sui built extensive walls, with Sui mobilizing over a million men.
- **Tang Dynasty (618–907)**: Focused on mobile campaigns rather than walls, viewing them as ineffective.
- **Northern Song (960–1127)**: Relied on existing walls as borders rather than new construction.
- **Liao, Western Xia, Jin, Yuan (907–1368)**: Jin built a 1,400 km network; Yuan did not focus on walls.
- **Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)**: Significant rebuilding in stone and brick, especially after the Tumu Crisis (1449) and Altan Khan raid (1550), forming today’s visible structure.
- **Qing Dynasty (1644–1911)**: Diminished defensive role, repurposed for movement control, gained Western fascination.
- **Modern China (1912–Present)**: Became a national symbol during Sino-Japanese conflict, faced damage during Cultural Revolution, now a UNESCO site and tourist destination with restoration efforts.

**Significance**: While effective against raids, the Wall failed major invasions like in 1644. It evolved from a criticized relic to a symbol of resilience, drawing millions to sites like Badaling.
