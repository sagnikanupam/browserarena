📄 Result: The Great Wall of China is an ancient series of walls and fortifications in northern China, stretching over 13,000 miles. It was initially conceived by Emperor Qin Shi Huang in the 3rd century B.C. to protect against barbarian nomads. The most well-known sections were constructed during the Ming Dynasty (14th-17th centuries A.D.), though it never fully prevented invasions. Its symbolic significance as a representation of Chinese strength endures.

**Historical Development:**
- **Qin Dynasty (221 B.C.)**: Emperor Qin Shi Huang initiated the wall to protect against northern attacks, connecting existing walls into a system spanning over 3,000 miles from Shanhaiguan to Gansu. Constructed with earth and stone, it involved a massive labor force, with up to 400,000 deaths reported during construction.
- **Post-Qin to Yuan Dynasty**: The wall fell into disrepair after the Qin Dynasty but was repaired and extended by the Northern Wei, Bei Qi (550-577 A.D., over 900 miles), and Sui Dynasties (581-618 A.D.). During the Tang Dynasty, it lost significance as China expanded. Under the Yuan (Mongol) Dynasty, it protected trade along the Silk Road.
- **Ming Dynasty (1368-1644 A.D.)**: The most extensive and preserved sections were built, especially after 1474, extending from the Yalu River to the Taolai River across multiple provinces. Strategic passes like Juyong and Yanmen were heavily fortified to defend Beijing.
- **Post-Ming Period**: The Manchus breached the wall in the 17th century, leading to the fall of the Ming Dynasty and the rise of the Qing Dynasty. From the 18th to 20th centuries, it became a symbol of Chinese strength to the Western world.
- **Modern Significance**: Recognized as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1987, it is one of humanity's greatest architectural feats. Though parts have deteriorated or been cut through for roadways, sections like Badaling (rebuilt in the 1950s) attract thousands of tourists daily. A myth that it is visible from space has been debunked by NASA.
