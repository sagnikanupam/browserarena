📄 Result: Sudden sharp chest pain can arise from a variety of thoracic processes, each with distinct mechanisms and tailored pharmacologic interventions. Below is a detailed overview of the key etiologies, their underlying pathology, and the primary drug therapies used to relieve symptoms and address the disease process.

1. Pleuritic Chest Pain
Pathophysiology: Inflammation of the parietal pleura (richly innervated by somatic nerves) releases inflammatory mediators into the pleural space, activating local pain receptors. Peripheral pleura involvement causes pain localized to chest wall dermatomes via intercostal nerves; diaphragmatic pleurisy refers to the neck or shoulder via the phrenic nerve.
Pharmacologic Management:
  • First-line: NSAIDs (e.g., indomethacin 50–100 mg PO TID) to reduce pleural inflammation and pain.
  • Corticosteroids: for NSAID-intolerant patients or tuberculous pleurisy (adjunctive therapy).
  • Colchicine: in familial Mediterranean fever–related pleurisy (1.2–2.0 mg daily).
  • Antimicrobials: when infection (pneumonia or parasitic pleuritis) is suspected.

2. Acute Pericarditis
Pathophysiology: Inflammation of the parietal pericardium stimulates its rich somatic innervation, producing severe retrosternal pain. Rapid pericardial fluid accumulation can cause tamponade by sharply increasing intrapericardial pressure.
Pharmacologic Management:
  • Idiopathic/viral: NSAIDs (ibuprofen 600 mg PO Q8H, indomethacin 25–50 mg PO Q8H, naproxen 500–1000 mg PO BID) plus colchicine (0.5–0.6 mg daily or BID) for 3–6 months.
  • Aspirin (500–1000 mg PO Q6–8H) in post-MI or pregnancy (first trimester).
  • Second-line: corticosteroids (prednisone 0.2–0.5 mg/kg/day) when NSAIDs/colchicine contraindicated, tapered slowly.
  • Refractory cases: immunosuppressives (azathioprine, IVIG, anakinra) or pericardiectomy.
  • Tuberculous pericarditis: standard anti-TB regimen plus adjunctive steroids in HIV-negative patients.

3. Myocardial Infarction (MI)
Pathophysiology: Coronary artery occlusion leads to myocardial ischemia and necrosis; visceral nerves transmit a deep, pressure-type pain often radiating to the jaw, shoulder, or arm.
Pharmacologic Management:
  • Antiplatelet therapy: aspirin 162–325 mg chewed immediately; add P2Y₁₂ inhibitor (clopidogrel or ticagrelor).
  • Nitrates: sublingual or IV nitroglycerin to reduce preload and alleviate ischemia (avoid in hypotension or right-ventricular MI).
  • Anticoagulation: unfractionated heparin or LMWH during acute phase.
  • Thrombolytics: if primary PCI is unavailable within guideline timeframes.
  • Beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors: early initiation to limit infarct size and remodeling.

4. Aortic Dissection
Pathophysiology: An intimal tear allows high-pressure blood to split the aortic wall layers, causing severe “tearing” chest or back pain and risk of branch vessel compromise.
Pharmacologic Management:
  • IV beta-blockers (e.g., esmolol) to reduce heart rate and aortic shear stress.
  • IV vasodilators (e.g., sodium nitroprusside) after adequate beta blockade to lower systolic BP to 100–120 mmHg.

5. Pulmonary Embolism (PE)
Pathophysiology: Thromboembolic obstruction of pulmonary arteries causes pleuritic pain by irritating the pulmonary pleura and can lead to right-ventricular strain.
Pharmacologic Management:
  • Anticoagulation: heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin in stable patients.
  • Systemic thrombolysis: for hemodynamically unstable (massive) PE.
  • Direct oral anticoagulants: in submassive or low-risk cases after initial parenteral therapy.

6. Spontaneous Pneumothorax
Pathophysiology: Air enters the pleural space, collapsing lung tissue and triggering sharp pleuritic pain via parietal pleura stretch.
Pharmacologic Management:
  • Analgesia (NSAIDs) and oxygen therapy for small, stable pneumothoraces.
  • No specific medications reverse the air leak—definitive management is needle aspiration or chest-tube drainage.

Summary
Sudden sharp chest pain encompasses diverse disorders—pleuritic inflammation, pericardial inflammation, ischemic myocardial injury, vascular wall disruption, pulmonary vascular occlusion, and intrapleural air. Each mechanism has a targeted pharmacologic regimen: anti-inflammatory agents for serosal inflammation; antiplatelet, anticoagulant, and reperfusion therapies for ischemic and thrombotic conditions; and hemodynamic stabilization in aortic dissection. Proper diagnosis guides selection of the most appropriate drug class, optimizing pain relief and addressing underlying pathology.
